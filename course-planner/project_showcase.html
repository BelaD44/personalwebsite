01 / The challenge

Make a research aircraft easier to change.

The existing research platform was based on the Sig Kadet LT-40 and used a wooden fuselage. My thesis explored a printable replacement that would make repairs and modifications easier while retaining the existing wing, tail surfaces, and propulsion type.

The fuselage had to do more than connect the aircraft’s major components. It also needed space for batteries, flight-control hardware, sensors, and experimental payloads—all while keeping the aircraft’s center of gravity within the intended range.

Project scope: fuselage concept and CAD design, print preparation and fit refinement, mass and center-of-gravity analysis, and static tests of selected printed assemblies.

02 / The architecture

Three segments. Defined interfaces.

A bolted panel structure makes individual parts accessible and replaceable. Flat panels also suit FDM printing because they require little or no support material. A square carbon-fiber tube replaces the wooden rear fuselage and carries the tail.

A / Front section

The motor-mount and nose-gear interfaces sit on the front panel. The interior houses heavy electronics and batteries to help balance the tail.

B / Central section

The wing spar passes through reinforced sidewalls. This section integrates flight-control hardware, the main landing-gear attachment, and a lower payload bay.

C / Tail support

A tapered structure supports a 20 mm square carbon boom. A split clamp restrains axial movement while the square geometry prevents rotation.

03 / Manufacturing

Designing for the printed part.

Printed prototypes turned nominal CAD dimensions into practical assembly clearances. The key lesson: a connection that fits on screen still needs manufacturing allowance.

From geometry to repeatable assembly

Initial 3.1 mm screw holes and 5.6 mm nut pockets were too tight. Testing led to 3.5 mm holes and 5.8 mm nut pockets for the M3 connections, allowing assembly without additional machining.

The baseline process used PLA, a 0.2 mm layer height, and 20% grid infill. The parts were sized around the available Prusa MK4S and Creality Ender 5 Plus printers.

Checking the mass estimates

Seven printed parts were weighed against PrusaSlicer estimates. The thesis reports a mean relative deviation of approximately 1.08%, supporting the use of slicer masses in the preliminary balance model.

04 / Mass & balance

Put the payload where it changes the balance least.

The 200 × 80 × 80 mm payload bay sits below the wing. In the CAD model, a uniformly distributed 1 kg payload changed the longitudinal center of gravity by just 0.25 mm, from 96.53 mm to 96.28 mm behind the wing’s leading edge.

05 / Physical validation

From CAD to the workbench.

Selected printed assemblies were loaded to check both the parts and their bolted connections. These tests established resistance to the applied static loads; they were not tests to failure.

What the tests establish

The selected assemblies carried these static test loads. Dynamic loads, vibration, resonance, and the complete fuselage—including the central wing attachment—still required further investigation.

06 / Outcome & next steps

A practical foundation for the next iteration.

The project produced a modular fuselage concept, printable parts, and initial physical evidence for the front and rear structures. The architecture supports changing a segment without rebuilding the entire fuselage.

Further work includes completing the tail-to-boom connection, validating the wing attachment and rear clamp, and testing the assembled aircraft under representative dynamic loads.

The engineering trade-off

Modularity came with a mass penalty.

The proposed fuselage and carbon boom totaled 1,162 g, compared with 880 g for the wooden fuselage—a 32% increase, excluding the wing and tail in both cases.

The first iteration prioritized functionality and structural confidence. The static results suggested room to reduce wall thickness and refine the print settings, but the thesis did not demonstrate a lighter final fuselage.
